In the last two lessons the first vowel changed into a consonant. When the first vowel is *a* or *ā*, that cannot happen: the a-class has no semivowel. Instead the two vowels fuse into one. Before Varadarāja can say into what, he needs a name for the result.

## The name guṇa

<SutraFolio id="1.1.2" level={2} />

Three vowels are called <Sa iast="guṇa" />: *a*, *e* and *o*. Split the sūtra and the *a* is <Sa iast="at" />, *a* with a *t* after it (<Sa iast="at" /> + <Sa iast="eṅ" />; the *t* is voiced to *d* before the vowel). That *t* matters. Varadarāja explains it with the very next sūtra he quotes.

<SutraFolio id="1.1.70" />

A vowel marked by *t* (written <Sa iast="tapara" />, "with t after it", or with t before it) names only vowels of **its own length**. On its own, *a* would name all eighteen varieties of the a-class (1.1.69), long *ā* included. As <Sa iast="at" /> it names only the short ones. So the guṇa vowels are short *a*, and *e* and *o*; long *ā* is not guṇa.

## a fuses with what follows

<SutraFolio id="6.1.87" />

<Sa iast="āt" /> is the ablative of *a*, "after a", and by 1.1.69 it covers *ā* too. The rule stands under a heading, <SutraRef id="6.1.84" />, <Sa iast="ekaḥ pūrvaparayoḥ" />: from here on, **one** substitute replaces **both** the earlier and the later sound. So *a* and the vowel after it are replaced together by a single guṇa vowel. Which one? 1.1.50 again picks the closest:

*upendraḥ* is "Upendra", Indra's younger brother, a name of Viṣṇu; *gaṅgodakam* is "Ganges water".

<Voice quote="vp-2.104" voice="darsana">
  Guṇa can make two different word sequences sound the same. Patañjali's example of a sentence with two meanings is *śveto dhāvati*: "the white one runs", or, read as *śvā ito dhāvati*, "a dog runs from here", where *ā* + *i* has fused into *e*. Bhartṛhari's own commentary on this verse explains it with *śveta*: in saṃhitā the sounds stay what they are, yet the one sound-sequence conveys both meanings.
</Voice>

<Voice quote="kas-6.1.87" voice="bhasya">
  The Kāśikā pairs a short and a long a with each vowel: *tava* + *idam*, *khaṭvā* + *indraḥ*, *tava* + *udakam*, *khaṭvā* + *udakam*, and so on through ṛ and ḷ. Its last examples, *tavalkāraḥ* and *khaṭvalkāraḥ*, show the l that follows a guṇa in place of ḷ.
</Voice>

## The r that rides with ṛ

Two sūtras explain *ar* and *al*. The first is LSK 28, a rule you might have expected in the Saṃjñā chapter.

<SutraFolio id="1.3.2" />

Varadarāja's gloss on it makes two points. Pāṇini's nasal vowels are not marked in writing: Pāṇini's followers know them from tradition (<Sa iast="pratijñānunāsikyāḥ pāṇinīyāḥ" />). And one of them, the *a* of the Śivasūtra <Sa iast="laṇ" />, is a marker. Taken together with the *r* just before it in <Sa iast="hayavaraṭ" />, it forms one more pratyāhāra, <Sa iast="ra" />, naming *r* and *l*.

<SutraFolio id="1.1.51" />

<Sa iast="uḥ" /> is "in place of ṛ", and since ṛ names thirty sounds (its own eighteen and the twelve of ḷ, as LSK 11 said), the rule covers ḷ as well. An *aṇ* vowel (a, i or u) that comes in place of ṛ comes in **already followed by ra**: by *r* in place of ṛ, by *l* in place of ḷ.

<Voice quote="kas-1.1.51" voice="bhasya">
  The phrase <Sa iast="prasajyamāna eva" />, "at the very moment it applies", matters: the substitute is never a bare *a* waiting for an r. The Kāśikā's examples come from inside words, where ṛ meets a suffix: *kartā* and *hartā* (guṇa *ar*), *kirati* and *girati* (*ir* from the roots *kṝ* and *gṝ*).
</Voice>

## The rule nobody can see

The last two sūtras of this stretch return to *e* and *o* at the end of a word. Varadarāja places them here because they need *guṇa* to make their point.

<SutraFolio id="8.3.19" />

After 6.1.78, *hare* + *iha* is *haray iha*, and *viṣṇo* + *iha* is *viṣṇav iha*. Now the *y* and the *v* stand at the end of a word, after *a*, before a vowel. In Śākalya's view they may be dropped. That leaves *hara iha* and *viṣṇa iha* — and a puzzle. Here is *a* before *i* again. Why does 6.1.87 not fuse them into *hareha*?

<SutraFolio id="8.2.1" />

Varadarāja's gloss states one of the deepest principles of the grammar. The Aṣṭādhyāyī has eight chapters of four quarters each. From 8.2.1 to the end, the last three quarters (the <Sa iast="tripādī" />) are **treated as not having happened** (<Sa iast="asiddha" />) as far as the first seven and a quarter chapters are concerned. Inside those three quarters, too, a later rule is invisible to an earlier one. 6.1.87 sits in chapter 6, 8.3.19 in chapter 8. To 6.1.87 the dropped *y* is still there, so there is no *a* + *i* for it to fuse.

<Voice quote="kas-8.3.19" voice="bhasya">
  The Kāśikā's examples show the same pair of forms with *ai* and *au*: *asmai* + *uddhara* gives *asmāyuddhara* or *asmā uddhara*, *dvau* + *atra* gives *dvāvatra* or *dvā atra*, *asau* + *ādityaḥ* gives *asāvādityaḥ* or *asā ādityaḥ*. In every case the vowels left facing each other stay apart.
</Voice>

<Voice quote="vp-2.238" voice="darsana">
  A rule that has applied, treated as if it had not: is the grammar describing something false? Bhartṛhari's answer covers every device of this kind. The means of teaching are ways of leading learners. One stands for a while on a path that is not itself the truth, in order to reach the truth: here, the two correct forms *harayiha* and *hara iha*.
</Voice>
