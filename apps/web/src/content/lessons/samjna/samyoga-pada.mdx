## Junction

<SutraFolio id="1.4.109" />

Varadarāja glosses <Sa iast="saṃhitā" /> as <Sa iast="varṇānām atiśayitaḥ sannidhiḥ" />, "the utmost nearness of sounds": sounds spoken one after another with no pause between them. This single term is the trigger for all of sandhi. The vowel rules of the next module stand under the heading <SutraRef id="6.1.72" />, <Sa iast="saṃhitāyām" />, "in saṃhitā", and apply only there.

So when must sounds be joined? A traditional verse that Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita quotes gives the answer.

<Voice quote="sk-samhita" voice="bhasya">
  Inside a word, between a prefix and its verb, and inside a compound, sandhi is compulsory. Between the words of a sentence it is the speaker's choice. That is why printed texts sometimes leave words unjoined, and why this course's sandhi tool asks whether your first word is a separate word.
</Voice>

<Voice quote="vp-2.58" voice="darsana">
  The phoneticians' phrase <Sa iast="padaprakṛtiḥ saṃhitā" /> can be read two ways, Bhartṛhari notes. Either the joined text has the separate words as its base, or the joined text is the base of the words, which analysis then cuts out of it. Pāṇini's rules work from words to the joined text. Bhartṛhari's own view, which you will meet in the next module, is that words are never fully separable from the sentence.
</Voice>

## Clusters

<SutraFolio id="1.1.7" />

Consonants with no vowel between them form a <Sa iast="saṃyoga" />. Varadarāja's gloss spells out the condition: <Sa iast="ajbhir avyavahitāḥ" />, "not separated by vowels". Examples:

- <Sa iast="agni" />: g and n
- <Sa iast="kṛṣṇa" />: ṣ and ṇ
- <Sa iast="indra" />: n, d and r, a three-consonant saṃyoga
- <Sa iast="ātman" />: t and m

The term matters right away for syllable weight. A short vowel is *light* (1.4.10 <Sa iast="hrasvaṃ laghu" />), but a short vowel before a saṃyoga counts as *heavy* (1.4.11 <Sa iast="saṃyoge guru" />). Meter and many rules of derivation depend on that distinction.

## Words

<SutraFolio id="1.4.14" />

This is the grammar's definition of a usable word, a <Sa iast="pada" />. Anything that ends in a nominal ending (<Sa iast="sup" />) or a verbal ending (<Sa iast="tiṅ" />) is one.

Notice what <Sa iast="sup" /> and <Sa iast="tiṅ" /> are: two more pratyāhāras. They are built by the same rule as <Sa iast="ac" /> and <Sa iast="hal" />, <SutraRef id="1.1.71" />, but from different upadeśa lists:

- **sup** runs from *su*, the first of the 21 nominal endings listed in 4.1.2, to the marker *p* of the last one.
- **tiṅ** runs from *tip*, the first of the 18 verbal endings listed in 3.4.78, to the marker *ṅ* of the last one.

So the method you learned on the Śivasūtras works on every list in the grammar.

<div class="example">
  <span><Sa iast="rāma" /> is a stem. It is not yet a word and cannot appear in a sentence.</span>
  <span><Sa iast="rāma" /> + <Sa iast="su" /> → <Sa iast="rāmaḥ" />: it now ends in a sup, so it is a pada.</span>
  <span><Sa iast="bhū" /> + <Sa iast="tip" /> → <Sa iast="bhavati" />: it ends in a tiṅ, so it is a pada.</span>
</div>

How *rāma + su* becomes *rāmaḥ*, and *bhū + tip* becomes *bhavati*, is the work of the rest of the course.

<Voice quote="mbh-sabda" voice="bhasya">
  Patañjali defines a word by what it does: "that which, once uttered, makes us understand" its meaning. Pāṇini's pada is the formal counterpart. Only a finished, inflected word can be uttered and understood. A bare stem or a bare ending cannot.
</Voice>
