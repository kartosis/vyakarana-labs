<SutraFolio id="1.1.9" />

Two sounds are <Sa iast="savarṇa" />, of one class, when they share **both** coordinates from the first lesson of this course: the place in the mouth (<Sa iast="āsya" />, glossed by Varadarāja as "palate and the rest") and the internal effort (<Sa iast="ābhyantara prayatna" />).

How many internal efforts are there? The Kāśikā names the four that count for savarṇa:

- **full contact** (<Sa iast="spṛṣṭa" />) for stops and nasals
- **slight contact** (<Sa iast="īṣatspṛṣṭa" />) for semivowels
- **open** (<Sa iast="vivṛta" />) for vowels, and on this count for the sibilants and h as well
- **closed** (<Sa iast="saṃvṛta" />) for short *a* as actually spoken. During a derivation, *a* is treated as open like the other vowels.

<Voice quote="kas-1.1.9-prayatna" voice="bhasya">
  The Kāśikā's list has four efforts. The vṛtti printed in the folio above, as the ashtadhyayi.com edition gives it, has five: it sets the sibilants apart as "slightly open" (<Sa iast="īṣadvivṛta" />), as the Siddhāntakaumudī does. Editions of the Laghusiddhāntakaumudī differ on this point. The chart below follows the Kāśikā's four, the count that the next rule, 1.1.10, presupposes.
</Voice>

A few results to notice. All five stops of a row form one class, including the nasal, because the nose is an extra passage rather than a place in the mouth. *i* and *ī* are one class, as are *a* and *ā*. A vārttika on this sūtra adds that *ṛ* and *ḷ* count as one class too.

<Voice quote="vp-1.47" voice="darsana">
  For Bhartṛhari a word is first shaped in the mind, then given body "by the sound that issues from the organs of speech." Places and efforts are those organs at work. Pāṇini's definition of savarṇa classifies sounds by exactly that bodily act.
</Voice>

## The tempting mistake

Now choose *i* on the chart. The palatal sibilant *ś* lights up with a dashed outline. Its place is the palate, and on the Kāśikā's four-fold count its effort is open, exactly like a vowel's. By 1.1.9 alone, *i* and *ś* would be savarṇa. The same goes for *a* and *h* (throat, open). The very next sūtra of the Aṣṭādhyāyī forbids it. Varadarāja leaves it out of the Laghusiddhāntakaumudī; Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita's fuller Siddhāntakaumudī keeps it as its thirteenth sūtra.

<Voice quote="kas-1.1.10" voice="bhasya">
  The Kāśikā shows what goes wrong without the rule. In *dadhi śītam* ("the curd is cold"), i and ś would merge into a long vowel. In *daṇḍa-hastaḥ* ("stick in hand"), a and h would. Neither happens: 1.1.10 keeps these vowels and consonants apart, even though their place and effort match.
</Voice>

## One letter for the whole class

This is LSK 11, and Varadarāja's gloss works out exactly how far each letter reaches.

<SutraFolio id="1.1.69" />

This is why the Śivasūtras could list only short *a*. In a rule, *a* stands for all eighteen of its varieties: short, long and prolated, at every pitch, nasal or not. Here *aṇ* takes the **second** ṇ (Varadarāja: <Sa iast="atraivāṇ pareṇa ṇakāreṇa" />, "only here is aṇ formed with the later ṇ"), so the principle reaches the semivowels *y v l* as well, which have nasal forms.

His gloss then counts what each letter covers. *a*, *i* and *u* name eighteen sounds each. *ṛ* names **thirty**: its own eighteen plus the twelve of *ḷ*, because the vārttika on 1.1.9 makes the two one class; *ḷ* likewise names thirty. The diphthongs *e ai o au*, which have no short forms, name twelve each.

The other half of the rule covers items marked with *u* (<Sa iast="udit" />). <Sa iast="ku" /> means the whole velar row *k kh g gh ṅ*, <Sa iast="cu" /> the palatal row, and so on through <Sa iast="ṭu" />, <Sa iast="tu" /> and <Sa iast="pu" />.

The last word sets a limit: <Sa iast="apratyayaḥ" />. Varadarāja explains <Sa iast="pratyaya" /> by its root sense, <Sa iast="pratīyate vidhīyata iti pratyayaḥ" />, "what is being prescribed". So the rule does not apply to a sound that a rule introduces — a suffix, a substitute or an augment. Such a sound stands for itself alone.

<Voice quote="vp-1.69" voice="darsana">
  Bhartṛhari reports a debate about exactly this kind of rule. When a sūtra names a sound, does it mean that individual sound, or the individual as known through its class? 1.1.69 is Pāṇini's practical answer for vowels and the five udit rows.
</Voice>
