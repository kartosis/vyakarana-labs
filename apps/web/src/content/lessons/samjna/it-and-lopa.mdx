Every list in the Śivasūtras ends with a consonant that is not a sound of the list. Pāṇini has to say so, or a reader would take *aiuṇ* to contain an ṇ. The first numbered rule of the Laghusiddhāntakaumudī does exactly that.

<SutraFolio id="1.3.3" />

**Upadeśa.** Varadarāja glosses it as <Sa iast="ādyoccāraṇam" />, "the first utterance": an item as Pāṇini states it when he teaches it. The Śivasūtras are <Sa iast="upadeśa" />, and so are the lists of roots, suffixes and substitutes you will meet later. In all of them, the final consonant is a marker.

**Carried-over words.** Two words of the rule's meaning, "in an upadeśa" and "is an it", are not in the rule at all. They come from the previous sūtra, <SutraRef id="1.3.2" />. That is why Varadarāja adds, on this very first rule, that a word missing from a sūtra is to be carried over from another, *everywhere*. You read that sentence in the folio above.

Commentators from Patañjali onward note a puzzle: the rule uses the name <Sa iast="hal" /> ("consonant") before the method of forming such names has been taught. The next lesson's rule supplies the method, and the tradition explains how the two rules support each other. For now, read *hal* simply as "consonant".

## Making a marker disappear

A marker is a tool for the grammarian, never a sound for the speaker. Two sūtras remove it. The first defines what "removal" means:

<SutraFolio id="1.1.60" />

The definition is careful. *Lopa* is not destruction but **non-appearance** (<Sa iast="adarśana" />) of something that was due to appear (<Sa iast="prasakta" />). The thing is still there for the grammar to refer to; it is simply not heard. The second sūtra applies it:

<SutraFolio id="1.3.9" />

<Voice quote="kas-1.3.9" voice="bhasya">
  Why does the rule need the word *tasya*, "of that"? Without it, a general principle (<SutraRef id="1.1.52" />) would delete only the last sound of whatever is to be removed. *Tasya* makes the whole marker disappear. Pāṇini's commentators read each syllable of a sūtra on the assumption that none is wasted.
</Voice>

Varadarāja's gloss adds a sentence that points forward: <Sa iast="ṇādayo 'ṇādyarthāḥ" />, "the markers ṇ and the rest are for the sake of aṇ and the rest." The markers vanish from speech, but they remain useful as name-tags.
