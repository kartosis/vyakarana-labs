The rule is short, and it is the key to reading Pāṇini. Take a sound from the Śivasūtras (the *ādi*, "beginning"). Put after it any marker that comes later (the *antya it*, "final marker"). The pair is a name, a <Sa iast="pratyāhāra" />, for that first sound and every sound up to the marker. Markers along the way are skipped, because they are not sounds.

## Build one by hand

**ac.** Start at *a* in line 1 and walk to the *c* that ends line 4: a, i, u, (skip ṇ), ṛ, ḷ, (skip k), e, o, (skip ṅ), ai, au. So <Sa iast="ac" /> = every vowel.

**hal.** Start at *ha* in line 5 and walk to the *l* that ends line 14. That is every consonant, with *ha* counted once although it appears twice.

**al.** From *a* to the last marker: every sound in the language.

Now try any pair on the board. Choose a first sound, then a red marker:

## When ṇ is ambiguous

ṇ closes two lines, 1 and 6, so <Sa iast="aṇ" /> could mean *a i u* or run on to *l*. The tradition settles each case. **aṇ** normally takes the first ṇ (a, i, u). In <SutraRef id="1.1.69" />, which you will meet soon, it takes the second. **iṇ** and **yaṇ** always use the second, since nothing else makes sense for them. Choose *a* on the board and then each red ṇ in turn to see the two readings.

<Voice quote="vp-1.50" voice="darsana">
  Every word, Bhartṛhari says, shows two things: its meaning and its own form. A pratyāhāra makes that distinction unavoidable. As a form, <Sa iast="ac" /> is a two-letter syllable. As a name, it means the nine vowels. Pāṇini later states the general principle: a word in grammar denotes its own form unless it is a technical name, <SutraRef id="1.1.68" />.
</Voice>
