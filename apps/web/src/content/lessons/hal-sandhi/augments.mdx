An <Sa iast="āgama" />, "augment", is a sound that a rule adds without removing anything. Pāṇini names most augments with a marker that says where they go, and the rule for reading those markers comes first.

## Where an augment goes

<SutraFolio id="1.1.46" level={2} />

An augment marked with *ṭ* goes at the **beginning** of the item it is added to; one marked with *k* goes at the **end**. So <Sa iast="kuk" /> (k, marked with k) attaches to the end of the earlier word, and <Sa iast="dhuṭ" /> (dh, marked with ṭ) to the beginning of the later one. The difference matters, as the next rule shows.

## k and ṭ before a sibilant

<SutraFolio id="8.3.28" />

After a word-final *ṅ* or *ṇ*, before a sibilant, the augment *k* or *ṭ* may be added. Varadarāja adds a vārttika on 8.4.48, <Sa iast="cayo dvitīyāḥ śari pauṣkarasāder iti vācyam" />: in the view of the grammarian Pauṣkarasādi, a first stop of a row (<Sa iast="cay" />: c ṭ t k p) before a sibilant becomes the second, its voiceless aspirate. Applied to the new k and ṭ, that gives three forms each:

<div class="example">
  <span><Sa iast="prāṅ" /> + <Sa iast="ṣaṣṭhaḥ" /> → <Sa iast="prāṅkh ṣaṣṭhaḥ" />, <Sa iast="prāṅk ṣaṣṭhaḥ" /> or <Sa iast="prāṅ ṣaṣṭhaḥ" /></span>
  <span><Sa iast="sugaṇ" /> + <Sa iast="ṣaṣṭhaḥ" /> → <Sa iast="sugaṇṭh ṣaṣṭhaḥ" />, <Sa iast="sugaṇṭ ṣaṣṭhaḥ" /> or <Sa iast="sugaṇ ṣaṣṭhaḥ" /></span>
</div>

<Voice quote="kas-8.3.28" voice="bhasya">
  Why does *kuk* attach to the end of the first word? The Kāśikā's first reason is the change of ś to ch. It reads 8.4.63 as applying only after a stop that ends a word, so the k must belong to *prāṅ*: *prāṅk chete*, "he lies facing east", beside *prāṅk śete* and *prāṅ śete*.
</Voice>

## t before s

<SutraFolio id="8.3.29" />

<SutraFolio id="8.3.30" />

After *ḍ*, and after *n*, an *s* may take the augment *dh* (<Sa iast="dhuṭ" />), which then loses its voice before the s by 8.4.55: <Sa iast="ṣaṭ tsantaḥ" /> or <Sa iast="ṣaṭ santaḥ" />; <Sa iast="san tsaḥ" /> or <Sa iast="san saḥ" />.

This time the marker is ṭ, so the augment begins the **second** word. The Kāśikā gives the reason: a dental after a word-final retroflex, like the ṭ of *ṣaṭ*, is protected by <SutraRef id="8.4.42" /> and stays dental. Had the augment ended the first word, the ṭ would no longer be word-final, and the t would turn retroflex.

## t before ś

<SutraFolio id="8.3.31" />

A word-final *n* before *ś* may take the augment *t* (<Sa iast="tuk" />), at its end for the same reason as *kuk*: so that ś may become ch after it. Three later rules then work on the junction, two of them optional, so Varadarāja lists **four** correct results for *san* + *śambhuḥ*, "the good Śambhu":

## The doubled nasal

<SutraFolio id="8.3.32" />

When a word ends in *ṅ*, *ṇ* or *n* after a **short** vowel, a following vowel takes the same nasal as an augment (<Sa iast="ṅamuṭ" />). The effect is a doubled nasal, and it is compulsory (<Sa iast="nityam" />):

<div class="example">
  <span><Sa iast="pratyaṅ" /> + <Sa iast="ātmā" /> → <Sa iast="pratyaṅṅ ātmā" />, "the inward self"</span>
  <span><Sa iast="sugaṇ" /> + <Sa iast="īśaḥ" /> → <Sa iast="sugaṇṇ īśaḥ" /></span>
  <span><Sa iast="san" /> + <Sa iast="acyutaḥ" /> → <Sa iast="sann acyutaḥ" /></span>
</div>

<Voice quote="kas-8.3.32" voice="bhasya">
  The Kāśikā's examples include *kurvann āste*, "he sits, working". Then it tests the words of the rule. Why "after a short vowel"? Because after a long one nothing is added: *prāṅ āste*, *bhavān āste*.
</Voice>

<Voice quote="vp-1.95" voice="darsana">
  An augment is a part added to a word. Bhartṛhari allows two accounts of parts: where words seem to have them, the difference in form comes from the sequence of sounds; where they have none, dividing them is a means of explaining them. Read with the second account, *sann acyutaḥ* is one continuous utterance, and the augment is the grammar's way of explaining the doubled n that speakers say.
</Voice>
